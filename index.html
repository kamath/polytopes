From polyhedra to polytopes

Nobody can picture four dimensions, and you don't need to.

Think of someone who lives inside a tabletop. They know left and right, forward and back. Up means nothing to them. Hold a cube over their world and the most they can ever get is a flat picture of it: a shadow, or a slice.

We are that person, one step up. There is a fourth direction we can't point in, and the most we can get of a 4D shape is a solid picture of it. So each figure below shows their view of a cube first, then our view of a tesseract, which is the 4D cube.

A tesseract is a cube, pulled once more

Start with a line. Pull it sideways and it leaves a square. Pull the square up and it leaves a cube. Pull the cube in the direction we can't point in.

A page has no "up", so a drawing fakes it with a slanted line. The blue lines fake the fourth direction the same way. Every pull doubles the corners: 2, 4, 8, 16.

A shadow is a flattened picture

Start with something ordinary. Hang a wire cube under a lamp and look at its shadow on the table.

The shadow is a square inside a square. The blue square is bigger only because the blue face is nearer the lamp. Both faces are the same size. Raise the lamp and the two shadows move closer to the same size.

A shadow is one step flatter than the thing

A flat square casts a shadow that is a line. A cube casts a shadow that is a flat shape. So a tesseract casts a shadow that is a solid shape. The tabletop is that person's whole world. For a lamp in the fourth direction, our whole room is the tabletop.

A line inside a line, a square inside a square, a cube inside a cube. We can't draw the tesseract or its lamp, only what lands in our room. Read it like the other two: the blue cube is not bigger and nothing is inside it. It is the side nearest the lamp.

Every piece of it is an ordinary cube

A cube's shadow has six pieces: two squares and four slanted shapes. All six are really squares. A tesseract's shadow has eight pieces: two cubes and six slanted blocks. All eight are really cubes. Step through them, and turn each one to face the lamp.

A shadow squashes whatever is side-on to the lamp. The slanted blocks are cubes seen side-on, the same way the slanted shapes on the left are squares seen side-on. Nothing about the tesseract changes when you turn it. Only its shadow does.

Laid out flat, they are eight plain cubes

A cardboard box flat-packs into six squares. A tesseract flat-packs into eight cubes. Pick a piece and find it in both pictures.

Nothing on the left is warped. Gluing the pieces back together is what takes an extra direction. A box needs "up". A tesseract needs the fourth direction, so nobody can build one in a room, and its shadow is the closest we get. These eight cubes are called its cells.

Passing through, it looks like a changing solid

The other flat picture is a slice, like one frame of a body scan. Push a cube through the tabletop and they see a flat shape that changes. Push a tesseract through our space and we see a solid that changes.

On the left, blue marks where each shape is being cut. On the right is that cut by itself. Three edges leave each corner of a cube, so it starts as a triangle. Four leave each corner of a tesseract, so it starts as a tetrahedron.

Only six perfectly regular ones exist

Regular means every side is the same shape, like a die. Try fitting copies of one shape around a point. A gap means they can fold up into the next dimension. No gap means they stay flat.

Five combinations leave a gap in 3D. Those are the Platonic solids. Six leave a gap in 4D.

Meet the six

Each one is the solid shadow that lands in our room, while the real shape slowly turns in 4D. One cell is marked in blue.

Read {4,3,3} left to right: squares, three per corner, make cubes. Then three cubes per edge make a tesseract.

When you get stuck

Lower every word by one dimension and ask what a flat creature would be confused about. It is usually the same thing, and you already know the answer.
