How do you draw a four-dimensional shape?

A tesseract is a cube dragged along a fourth direction, the way a cube is a square dragged along a third.

It has four dimensions and the screen has two, so a picture of one has lost two dimensions. A cube drawn on a line has lost two as well, and that is something we can watch happen. This page does it to the cube first and then to the tesseract.

First, a cube from 3D to 2D

Hang a cube under a lamp and it casts a shadow on the sheet below. The shadow is flat, so it has one dimension fewer than the cube.

The shadow has four sides or six depending on how the cube is turned. The cube itself stays the same.

Then the same cube from 2D to 1D

Lay a second lamp on the sheet and the flat shadow lands on the sheet's edge as a bar. The cube is now two dimensions down.

A lot is lost on the way. All that is left of the cube is a bar that gets longer and shorter as the cube turns.

Now a tesseract from 4D to 2D

A tesseract takes the same two steps. Its shadow is solid, in 3D, and the screen then flattens that to 2D.

It loses about as much as the cube did on its way to a line, which is why the picture looks warped. A flat screen can't do better than this.

Blue marks one of the tesseract's eight cubes, which are called its cells.

There is no lamp in the picture because the lamp is off in the fourth direction, where we can't point.

Why do some of the cells look bigger or squashed?

A cell that is nearer the lamp casts a bigger shadow.

The blue cell isn't really bigger than the small one, and the small one isn't inside it. Raise the lamp and the two even out.

The six cells in between look squashed because they are side-on to the lamp.

Turn one to face the lamp and it straightens out, into a plain square on the left and a plain cube on the right. All eight cells are the same cube.

Can you build one?

A box cut along its edges opens into six flat squares, and they fold back up into the box. A tesseract opens into eight cubes and folds back up from them.

At "flat" the eight cubes sit in our space as they are. The fold goes in the fourth direction, so from there on we see its shadow, where the lid grows as it swings toward the lamp and the sides slant. This fold is what the visualizer plays.

Why are there only six of these?

Flat pieces can fold only if there is a gap to close. Three squares around a corner leave a gap, so they fold into the corner of a cube. Four leave none, so they lie flat.

A shape is regular when all of its pieces are alike and meet the same way everywhere. With polygons around a corner, five arrangements leave a gap, and those are the five regular solids. Switch the figure to 4D, where solids fit around an edge, and six do.

Here are all six, each seen as its shadow.

The 120-cell is made of 120 dodecahedra, the solid with twelve pentagon faces, with three around every edge. They look like different sizes because of the lamp, and all 120 are identical to the blue one.

The code by each name is its recipe. {5,3,3} means five-sided faces, three at each corner, then three of those solids around each edge.

So what am I looking at?

A 4D shape that has lost two dimensions, the way a cube drawn on a line has. A lamp we can't point to casts its solid shadow into our space, and the screen flattens that to 2D. Its cells look bigger or squashed depending on where they sit, and they are all identical.

The shape is also one of only six, and in the visualizer each of them folds up from its cells. Three dimensions allow five regular shapes and four allow six. Every dimension after that allows only three.
