A smaller version of the same problem turns up every time a solid thing is drawn on a flat sheet of paper.

What does 4D even mean?

A dimension is an independent axis: a direction to measure along that none of the others can stand in for. A line has one, its length. A square has two, and a cube has three. The world we live in stops at three, but mathematics doesn't. A fourth axis can be added, then a fifth, and so on without end. A tesseract is what a cube becomes with a fourth.

The animation rests on each shape and picks out in blue what it is made of: the square itself, then the cube's six squares one at a time, then the tesseract's eight cubes one at a time.

Each drag goes along a new axis, and this is what independent means: moving along a line never takes you sideways, and sliding around on a sheet never takes you up. Our space has no fourth axis to drag the cube along, so the drawing fakes one with the blue slanted lines, the same way a flat page fakes "up" to draw a cube.

Projecting into a lower dimension

A shape can't fit into a space with fewer dimensions than it has, but it can leave a picture of itself there. A shadow is the everyday case. Here a cube hangs between a lamp and a sheet of paper, and the right-hand panel shows its shadow by itself: a three-dimensional shape, projected into two dimensions.

The shadow is flat. It has an outline and nothing more, yet it changes as the cube turns: four sides when a face points at the lamp, six when a corner does. The cube itself never changes.

This is called a projection: the picture a shape leaves in a space with one dimension fewer. Something is always lost, since a shadow has no depth, but it is the only way a shape can show up in a space too small to hold it.

From four dimensions to three

A tesseract is the four-dimensional cube, built from eight cubes the way a cube is built from six squares. Our space is one dimension too small to hold it, so it can only show up here as a projection. The lamp and the tesseract sit out in the fourth direction, and the shadow lands in our three-dimensional space, which plays the part of the sheet. The figure shows what arrives.

The blue cell is a perfect cube on the tesseract, but in the projection it is a lopsided block, and it keeps changing as the tesseract turns even though the tesseract stays the same.

Where is the lamp in 4D?

A flat creature living in a tabletop can only be shown a cube as a shadow on its table. With a tesseract, the 4D cube, we are that creature, and the whole of our three-dimensional space is the table. A shadow always has one dimension fewer than the thing that casts it: a square's shadow is a line, a cube's is a flat shape, and a tesseract's is a solid shape that lands right here in our space. The three pictures show those in order.

The third picture has no lamp in it. To see why, look at the middle picture from the creature's side. Its lamp hangs above the table, which is a place the creature can't see or even point to. All that ever reaches it is the shadow. We are in the same position. The lamp that shines on the tesseract sits off in the fourth direction, outside our space, and only the shadow arrives.

The lamp also accounts for the sizes. Whatever is nearer the lamp casts a bigger shadow, the way the shadow of a hand grows as it moves toward a flashlight. So the blue cube isn't really bigger than the small one, and the small one isn't inside it. The blue one is the side nearest the lamp. Raise the lamp and the two get closer in size.

Are the slanted blocks really cubes?

The shadow of a tesseract, the 4D cube, is a small cube inside a big one with six lopsided blocks in between. Each of those six is an ordinary cube that happens to be side-on to the lamp. A book held side-on to a flashlight casts a thin sliver of a shadow for the same reason.

The right-hand picture is that shadow, with one of its eight pieces in blue. Step through the pieces, and use the slider to turn the tesseract until the blue one faces the lamp. Its shadow straightens out into a plain cube. The left-hand picture does the same one level down, on the flat shadow of a cube.

On the left, every piece turns out to be a square. A cube has only six of them, so the last two steps mark nothing there. On the right, all eight pieces turn out to be cubes. Nothing about the tesseract changes as you turn it. Only its shadow does.

Folding, seen as projections

Six squares in a flat cross fold up into a box. The first panel shows that happening, and the second shows the shadow it casts on a sheet. The third shows the same fold one dimension up: eight cubes folding into a tesseract, as the shadow it casts in our space. Pick a piece and follow it through all three.

Whichever piece is picked, it starts out as a plain cube. Folded up, the one that ends farthest from the lamp becomes the small inner cube, the one nearest it becomes the big outer cube, and the six left side-on become slanted blocks.

The first panel settles what really happens to the squares: they swing upward, and none of them is squashed. The second is all that a flat creature living in the sheet would see of it. The third puts us in that creature's place, and it follows the same rules. The cubes that appear crushed are turning side-on to the lamp, and the one that appears to swell is swinging toward it.

The squares that wrap a cube are called its faces. The cubes that wrap a tesseract are called its cells.

Slices

A shadow is one way to show a shape to someone with fewer dimensions, and a slice is another. Push a cube straight through a tabletop, and a flat creature living in the table would watch the cross-section, the way a medical scanner shows a body one slice at a time. It would see a flat shape appear, change and vanish. If a tesseract, the 4D cube, passed through our space, we would see a solid shape do the same. The top row shows the cube passing through the table and, beside it, the flat slice the creature sees. The bottom row shows the tesseract passing through our space and the solid slice we would see.

Blue marks where each shape is being cut, and the panel beside it shows that cut by itself. The cube here goes through corner first, so the creature sees a small triangle that grows into a hexagon and shrinks back. We would see a small pyramid with four triangular sides, called a tetrahedron, which grows, has its corners cut off until it is an eight-faced octahedron at the halfway point, and then shrinks back.

It doesn't have to be cubes

A cube is not the only shape that folds up from flat pieces. To see which others do, ask why a flat cross of squares can fold at all. Lay three squares around a single corner and they don't go all the way round. There is a gap, and folding is what closes it. Lay four around a corner and there is no gap, so they sit flat like floor tiles. You can try other shapes here.

If you insist that every face be the same shape and every corner look the same, only five arrangements leave a gap, so only five solids can be built this way. The ancient Greeks knew all five. One of them puts three pentagons at each corner and closes up after twelve. It's called a dodecahedron.

Now switch the figure to 4D. It's the same game one level up: fit solid pieces around an edge and see whether there is a gap to fold into. This time six arrangements work. One of them is three dodecahedra around each edge, and that one is the 120-cell.

The 120-cell, and the other five

These are the only six perfectly regular shapes in four dimensions. Each is shown as the shadow it casts in our space while it slowly turns in 4D, with one cell, meaning one of its solid pieces, in blue.

The 120-cell is 120 dodecahedra, the solid with twelve pentagon faces, joined face to face with three around every edge and folded shut in the fourth direction. Because the picture is a shadow, cells nearer the lamp come out larger, cells farther from it come out smaller, and cells side-on to it are squashed nearly flat. In the real shape all 120 are identical to the blue one.

The code next to each name is a recipe. {5,3,3} means five-sided faces, three of them at each corner, and then three of the resulting solids around each edge. If you'd like to watch any of the six being folded up from its cells, the visualizer does that.

There is one thing here I find remarkable. Three dimensions allow five perfectly regular shapes, and four dimensions allow six. The number doesn't keep growing. In five dimensions and in every dimension beyond, there are only three.

One dimension down

There is one move that works on almost any picture of four dimensions. Take the whole situation down one dimension: imagine a flat creature living in a tabletop, looking at the shadow of an ordinary solid, and ask what it could and couldn't tell from that shadow. The same limits apply to us one level up, and from outside the table the reason for each one is visible.
