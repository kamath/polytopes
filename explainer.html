Most of the figures can be dragged to turn them. Back to the visualizer

You can't picture a fourth dimension. Hardly anyone can. You can still draw a four-dimensional shape and read the drawing, with a trick you have known since you were small: shadows.

What is a tesseract?

Drag a line sideways and you get a square. Drag the square up and you get a cube. Drag the cube in a direction that isn't any of the three you know, and you get a tesseract.

The last step is no harder to say than the first two. It is only harder to see.

How do you draw one?

A tesseract has four dimensions and your screen has two, so any picture of it has thrown two away. That sounds hopeless until you try it on a cube, which ends up on a line.

First, a cube from 3D to 1D

Hang a cube under a lamp and its shadow on the sheet below is flat. One dimension is gone. Lay a second lamp on the sheet, and that shadow casts a shadow of its own onto the sheet's edge, as a bar.

Turn the cube. It never changes, only its shadows do. Two dimensions down, all that is left is a bar, and nobody would guess it came from a cube.

How much of the cube can we get back?

More than you would think. A shadow is a simple rule: each point slides along a straight ray from the lamp until it hits something. So follow the cube's corners down one at a time. One side is in blue.

The line now has eight marks, one for each corner, and the four blue ones are one face. What you could read off the cube, you can still read off the line. It just takes more effort.

Watch the marks as the cube turns. They cross and spread apart, which nothing living on a line could do. Something is turning in directions the line doesn't have.

Now a tesseract, from 4D to 2D

The tesseract takes the same two steps. A lamp leaves its shadow in our space, and the screen flattens that. Every picture of a tesseract, anywhere, has lost two dimensions.

A computer draws it the way we followed the cube: sixteen corners, each just four numbers, the same rule twice, then join the dots. You don't have to picture the shape. You only have to follow the rule.

Here they are turning side by side. On the left a square stretches, slants and squares up again, and you simply see a cube turning. The right is doing the same thing one dimension up, with eight cubes, called cells, where the cube has six squares.

Nothing bent the blue cell. Facing the lamp it is a plain cube, and all eight cells are the same cube. The picture looks warped because it is two dimensions short of what it shows.

On the left, your eye puts the missing dimension back without being asked. On the right it can't, so the same turn looks like a cube swelling and changing shape. That strangeness is the fourth dimension showing.

Why do some of the cells look bigger?

The slant explains the squashed cells, but not why one looks so much bigger than another. That part is the lamp. Anything nearer to it casts a bigger shadow.

On the right, a small cube seems to sit inside a big one. Don't believe it. They are the same size, and one is just closer to the lamp. Raise the lamp and they even out.

Can you build one?

You can also build one. Four sticks in a line fold into a square, and that fold needs a second dimension. Six squares fold into a box, which needs a third. So eight cubes should fold into a tesseract, given a fourth.

At "flat" the eight cubes are sitting in our space. The fold goes in the fourth direction, so we watch its shadow. This is the fold the visualizer plays, and it has a catch: pieces can only fold if there is a gap to close.

Why are there only six?

Three squares around a corner leave a gap, so they fold into the corner of a cube. Four leave none and lie flat. That decides which regular shapes can exist, the ones whose pieces are all alike and meet the same way everywhere.

With polygons around a corner, only five arrangements leave a gap, and those are the five regular solids. Switch the figure to 4D, where solids fit around an edge, and six do. There is no seventh.

What do the six look like?

Here they are, each one as its shadow.

In the 120-cell, all 120 dodecahedra are identical to the blue one. The lamp makes them look different sizes.

The code by each name is its recipe. {5,3,3} means five-sided faces, three at each corner, then three of those solids around each edge.

So what am I looking at?

A shadow of a shadow. A lamp we can't point to casts the shape's shadow into our space, and the screen flattens it once more. Its cells look warped and unequal, and they are all identical.

And one of only six. Three dimensions allow five regular shapes and four allow six. You might expect five to allow seven. It allows three, and so does every dimension after that.
